Borrowed Time is an action roguelike where your remaining lifespan powers everything you do.  
You begin each run with 10 minutes to live. A clock embedded in your wrist counts down in real time. You’re trying to reach the Clockmaker, who holds the years stolen from you—but every shortcut and supernatural ability brings death closer.  
The central decision  
Walking and basic attacks only cost the time naturally passing. Powerful abilities spend extra:  
| Ability | Cost | Effect |  
|---|---|---|  
| Blink | 5 seconds | Teleport past danger or through a barrier |  
| Second Chance | 20 seconds | Rewind your position and health by three seconds |  
| Borrowed Strength | 15 seconds | Deal double damage for a short burst |  
| Stillness | 10 seconds | Freeze enemies and hazards for four seconds |  
| Future Sight | 8 seconds | Reveal traps, enemy intentions, and hidden routes |  
Good play means knowing when spending time will save more than it costs.  
How a run plays  
Explore a shifting city, fight its time collectors, and choose routes toward the Clockmaker’s tower. Each district offers a different gamble: a safe but lengthy passage, a dangerous shortcut, or an optional job that might pay enough time to fund the rest of your run.  
You earn time through precise combat, discoveries, and contracts. Enemies give fixed rewards, so dragging out a fight is expensive. A clean parry or clever environmental kill can make a dangerous encounter profitable.  
Between districts, you reach a sanctuary where the clock pauses. You can consider upgrades and plan your next move without rushing. Menus and dialogue also pause the countdown.  
The world  
Time has become currency. The rich live for centuries while everyone else trades away tomorrow to survive today. Street vendors sell bottled minutes. Debt collectors literally take years off people’s lives.  
Deals give that premise teeth:  

- A surgeon offers more maximum health for one minute.
- A stranger needs 30 seconds to reach home. Helping them could open a route later.
- A lender gives you two minutes now but demands three at the next checkpoint.
- A locked vault takes 40 seconds to crack—or five seconds to blink into, provided you know what’s inside.

Builds that change your relationship with the clock  
A Duelist earns bonus seconds through perfect parries. A Debtor uses powerful abilities on credit, then must collect enough time before repayment. A Scavenger reveals forgotten caches and profits from exploration. A Last Breath build becomes stronger below one minute, rewarding players who stay dangerously close to zero.  
Permanent progression unlocks abilities, routes, and characters. Your starting lifespan stays consistent so the pressure remains meaningful.  
The final twist  
When you reach the Clockmaker, defeating them isn’t enough. You must decide what to do with the time you recover: keep it, return it to the city, or spend it undoing one event from your past. The people you helped determine which endings are possible.  
For a first playable version, I’d build one district, three abilities, two enemy types, one time lender, and a boss. The test is whether spending five precious seconds to pull off a brilliant move feels exciting enough to do again.

Your game could work around one rule: the comic can’t move forward until you make its unfinished scenes happen. You play a character who wakes up inside a rough pencil sketch, discovers the next page is blank, and starts searching for the missing ending.  
I’d make it a puzzle adventure where you explore inside panels and edit parts of the page. That gives you two connected ways to play: being in the story and helping create it.  
The main gameplay loop  

1. Enter an unfinished page. Some panels are complete; others have missing objects, dialogue, or backgrounds.
2. Explore the panels. Talk to characters, inspect sketches, and figure out what’s stopping the scene.
3. Find comic elements. Collect a word, drawing, sound effect, or piece of another panel.
4. Use them to finish the action. Place or change an element, then play through what happens.
5. Complete the page. The finished scene gains ink and color, revealing the way forward.

Every page should have a clear story problem—“escape the rooftop,” “convince the guard,” “find the missing friend”—so completing it feels like an adventure.  
Your main abilities  
Introduce these gradually, with each chapter adding a new way to change the comic.  
| Ability | How it works | Example puzzle |  
|---|---|---|  
| Draw | Complete marked, unfinished objects | Finish a ladder so you can climb it |  
| Erase | Remove certain inked details | Erase a chain holding a gate shut |  
| Move words | Transfer words between speech bubbles or captions | Move “asleep” into a guard’s description |  
| Place sound effects | Make comic sounds affect the world | Put CRASH! on a cracked floor to break it |  
| Rearrange panels | Change the order of events | Make rain fall before a character lights a fuse |  
For drawing, you could let the player trace a faint outline or choose from a few sketches. Freehand drawing could be optional, depending on how much you want artistic skill to matter.  
An example playable page  
The situation: Your character needs to escape a rooftop before an ink creature catches them.  
The page has four panels:  

- Panel 1: You arrive on the rooftop. A loose WHOOSH! floats beside an unfinished clothesline.
- Panel 2: There’s a huge gap between buildings.
- Panel 3: A billboard hangs above the gap, but its support is cracked.
- Panel 4: Mostly blank, showing only a doorway on the opposite rooftop.

The solution:  
You complete the clothesline and climb it to reach the billboard. Then you move WHOOSH! beneath the sign, making it swing across the gap. You jump onto it, reach the other roof, and enter the doorway.  
As you succeed, Panel 4 draws itself around you. The page fills with color, and the ink creature arrives one panel too late.  
That teaches the player that unfinished art and comic lettering are usable parts of the world.  
How the story could develop  
| Chapter | What happens | New mechanic |  
|---|---|---|  
| 1. Rough Draft | You wake up and try to discover who you are | Complete drawings |  
| 2. Missing Words | Characters have lost their dialogue and motivations | Move words |  
| 3. Out of Order | Events happen in the wrong sequence | Rearrange panels |  
| 4. The Gutter | You enter the white space between panels and find abandoned scenes | Travel between disconnected panels |  
| 5. The Last Page | You discover why the comic was abandoned and create its ending | Combine everything |  
An emotional direction that fits: the creator stopped drawing because they couldn’t decide how the story should end. Characters have been waiting in unfinished moments ever since. Some want their intended ending; others want a chance to change it.  
Your character might even be a discarded side character who was never supposed to reach the final page.  
What makes the puzzles feel fair  
The biggest design challenge is explaining why the player can edit some things but not everything. Establish a consistent visual language:  

- Pencil outlines can be completed.
- Loose lettering can be moved.
- Smudged ink can be erased.
- Solid finished ink stays fixed.

Let players preview changes and undo mistakes quickly. An unexpected result can be funny or reveal a clue without forcing them to restart the whole page.  
You can also allow occasional alternate solutions. A blocked doorway might open through a loud BANG!, or you could finish a window and climb through it. Those choices can change small details in the completed comic.  
A good first version to build  
Start with three connected pages and only two mechanics: completing drawings and moving sound effects.  

- Page 1: Finish an object to learn how editing works.
- Page 2: Move a sound effect to solve an obstacle.
- Page 3: Combine both mechanics during an escape.

Use one main character, one supporting character, and one pursuing ink creature. Finish with a page-turn reveal.  
Your first design target: make completing a page feel satisfying—the sketch becomes a finished comic page, and the player can see exactly how their actions created the scene.
